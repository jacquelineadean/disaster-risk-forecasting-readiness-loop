The Readiness Loop — Research Report
Research briefing August 5, 2026 · 21 sources · US-first, global by design

The Readiness Loop

An open-source, agentic system that forecasts natural-disaster risk from public data, validates its own probabilities against history, and iterates until they can be trusted — then turns them into emergency plans.

This report maps the terrain for the project: what already exists (Google's forecasting stack, open risk models, two decades of open hazard data), where the genuine gap is, how the Claude Agent SDK's agent loop fits the experimental cycle you described, how to define "accurate enough" honestly, and a five-phase roadmap from a single-hazard prototype to a global planning thought-partner. Every load-bearing claim links to a numbered source at the end.

How to read this

Findings are stated in the main column; margin notes carry sources [n] and caveats. The roadmap in §6 is the part meant to be iterated on.
Headline takeaways
1

The pieces exist; the product doesn't. Open data now covers US hazard history back to 1950, every building footprint in the country, county-level baseline risk, and AI weather forecasts NOAA itself runs operationally. No open project wires them into a self-validating forecasting loop with a planning layer on top. [6][10][11][15]

2

The Claude Agent SDK's loop is the same shape as your experiment. Anthropic describes its agents as running gather context → take action → verify work → repeat. That maps one-to-one onto fetch data → model risk → backtest against history → recalibrate until a threshold is met. [4][5]

3

Don't invent physics — orchestrate it. CLIMADA (ETH Zurich, open source) already computes probabilistic risk as hazard × exposure × vulnerability, globally, on a laptop. The agent's edge is automating the calibrate-validate-iterate cycle around such engines, not replacing them. [7]

4

Google Open Buildings does not cover the United States. Its 1.8B detections span Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean. For the US-first phase, exposure comes from Microsoft's 129.6M footprints, FEMA/ORNL USA Structures, and Overture; Open Buildings is the unlock at global scale-out. [3][11][12][13]

5

"Accuracy within a threshold" must mean calibration, not hit rate. The standard toolkit — Brier score, reliability diagrams, skill relative to climatology, locked holdout years — exists precisely so an iterating forecaster can't fool itself. Adopt it wholesale. [19]

6

Memorial Hospital is the argument for the planning layer. Katrina was tracked for days; 45 bodies were still carried out of one hospital. That was a preparedness failure, not a forecast failure — and it defines what the thought-partner must eventually stress-test. [8][9]

7

Federal data is load-bearing and not guaranteed. NOAA retired its Billion-Dollar Disasters product in May 2025 with no updates beyond 2024. An open-source project should snapshot and mirror what it depends on — that is part of the mission, not overhead. [20]

01 · The gap

Warnings have gotten remarkably good. Readiness hasn't kept up.

The forecasting layer you'd have had to build a decade ago now largely exists, much of it from the Google work you flagged. Flood Hub issues riverine flood forecasts up to seven days ahead and covers about two billion people in more than 150 countries, with flash-flood forecasts a day out; Google has open-sourced a global flash-flood dataset and the hydrology framework behind it. WeatherNext 2 produces hourly global forecasts in minutes, and its output — which predicted Hurricane Melissa's Jamaica landfall roughly five days ahead — is used by the National Hurricane Center. FireSat is being built to detect fires at 5×5 meters with ~20-minute revisits. Android phones double as a global seismometer network. Google's stated north star: no one should be surprised by a natural disaster. [1]

But whether people survive a disaster is mostly decided before it arrives: whether the hospital's plan contemplated losing power for four days, whether the county has evacuation triggers written down, whether anyone rehearsed. That analytical work is concentrated in well-resourced jurisdictions that can afford risk consultants and full-time emergency managers. Small counties, rural hospital systems, and most of the Global South cannot — which is precisely the population your application targets.

The precedent that forecasts only matter when wired to pre-committed action is already established: Google's partnerships with GiveDirectly and the IRC send cash to families in Nigeria and Bangladesh before forecast floods arrive. [1] The open niche is the layer between public data and local decisions: a system any county, hospital, or NGO can run, inspect, and extend — producing calibrated local risk numbers first, and rehearsable plans second.

Google's ForestCast work is a useful template for the modeling posture: it reframed deforestation from monitoring losses to forecasting risk, deliberately used satellite data alone so the method scales to regions with no ground truth, and shipped an open benchmark dataset so others could iterate. Forecast from open data, publish the benchmark, let the community push accuracy — the same posture fits disaster risk. [2]

"No one should be surprised by a natural disaster."

Google's stated goal for its crisis-resilience work, June 2026 [1]

Why this stays open source

Trust in a risk number depends on being able to audit how it was made. Closed property-risk models are repeatedly criticized as black boxes; an open pipeline with published backtests is the differentiator, and the only version under-resourced governments can actually adopt.
02 · Case study

Memorial Medical Center: what a preparedness failure looks like

When Katrina's floodwaters cut off Memorial Medical Center in Uptown New Orleans in August 2005, the storm itself had been watched for days. The hospital lost power and running water; interior temperatures climbed past 100°F; staff and patients were marooned for four days. [9] Mortuary workers eventually carried 45 bodies from Memorial — more than from any comparable-size hospital in the flooded city. Tenet, the hospital's owner, reported 34 patient deaths on the campus, 24 of them in the long-term acute-care unit run by LifeCare. [8]

What followed is the part that should shape this application. Sheri Fink's Pulitzer-winning investigation documented exhausted clinicians improvising triage — including decisions about which patients would be moved last, and allegations that some were given lethal doses of sedatives — under conditions the institution's emergency plan had never rehearsed. A physician and two nurses were arrested in 2006 in connection with four deaths; a grand jury declined to indict in 2007. Statewide, investigators reviewed roughly 215 deaths at hospitals and nursing homes after the storm. [8][9]

The lesson is precise: the deaths at Memorial were not caused by a missing forecast. They were caused by a plan that assumed short outages, working infrastructure, and prompt evacuation — assumptions nobody had stress-tested against the region's known flood risk. That is the gap a planning thought-partner attacks, and it is a tractable one, because the failure was analytical, not meteorological.

What a thought-partner would have asked Memorial in 2004

→Your county's flood scenarios put N feet of water at this address. Where, physically, is your electrical switchgear relative to that line — and what fails when it goes under?
→What is the written trigger for evacuating before landfall, who has authority to pull it, and what is the patient priority order — sickest first or last?
→Run the 96-hour isolation drill: no grid, no water, no resupply, 2,000 people in the building. Where does the plan break first?
→Which receiving facilities have signed transfer agreements, and do they flood in the same scenarios you do?

Every question is answerable from data in §3 plus the facility's own records — grounded, checkable, and specific to one address. This is the Phase 3 product in miniature.

03 · The data landscape

Everything the loop needs is public. Here is the shopping list.

A risk forecast needs four layers — hazard history for ground truth, exposure (what's in harm's way), vulnerability and baseline risk, and live forecasts — plus an engine to combine them. Each layer has a strong open default for the US, and a designated swap for global scale-out.

A · Ground truth & hazard history — the validation target

NOAA Storm Events Database
NWS / NCEI
The official record: 48 event types at county/zone level from January 1950 to the present, with injuries, fatalities, and property and crop damage per event. This is the primary series the loop backtests against. [10]
Free bulk CSV; updated monthly with ~120-day lag. Caveat: collection practice changed over the decades — period of record varies by event type.
Billion-Dollar Disasters archive
NOAA NCEI · retired
Normalized major-loss events 1980–2024 (~400 events). Retired May 2025 with no updates beyond calendar 2024 — useful frozen context for large-event costs, not a living series. [20]
Archive still downloadable. Snapshot it early; scientists note it cannot easily be reconstructed elsewhere.
Global analogs
for scale-out
EM-DAT (CRED's international disaster database) and national civil-protection records play the Storm Events role outside the US; coarser, but sufficient for country-level calibration in later phases.
Free for research with registration.

B · Exposure — what is standing in harm's way

US Building Footprints
Microsoft
129,591,852 computer-generated footprints for all 50 states, extracted from satellite imagery. The default US exposure base. [11]
Free GeoJSON by state; ODbL license (share-alike — see §7 licensing note).
USA Structures
FEMA · Oak Ridge National Lab
125M+ structures over 450 sq ft for the US and territories, with occupancy-type attribution — built specifically to support disaster response. Attribution makes it the better join for "how many homes, hospitals, schools are exposed." [12]
Free via FEMA Geospatial hub / Living Atlas; checked annually.
Overture Maps buildings
Linux Foundation consortium
1.3B+ footprints conflated from OpenStreetMap, Microsoft, Esri and others into one schema with stable IDs — the cleanest single global schema to build the pipeline against. [13]
Free Parquet releases; query with DuckDB straight from cloud storage.
Google Open Buildings
Google Research
1.8B building detections across 58M km² of Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, with confidence scores; the 2.5D Temporal variant adds height and change over time. Purpose-built for exactly the under-mapped regions the global phase targets. [3]
Free (CC BY-4.0 / ODbL), incl. Earth Engine. No US coverage — a common planning mistake; see margin.

C · Vulnerability & baseline risk — the prior

National Risk Index
FEMA
18 natural hazards scored for every US county and census tract, combining expected annual loss, social vulnerability, and community resilience. v1.20 shipped December 2025. Use it as the prior and the benchmark to beat — but its scores are relative rankings, not probabilities, which is exactly the gap this project fills. [6]
Free CSV/shapefile via OpenFEMA; versioned archive available.
First Street aggregates
First Street
Peer-reviewed property-level flood/wildfire/heat/wind models with public methodology. Property-level data is commercial, but aggregated summary statistics are free for noncommercial use — a useful independent comparison for your county-level outputs. [18]
Aggregates via AWS Open Data / Living Atlas; property level is paid.

D · Live forecasts & reforecasts — the dynamic signal

AIGFS / GraphCast-GFS
NOAA NCEP · Google DeepMind
GraphCast — open code and weights, 10-day global forecasts in under a minute, beating the ECMWF gold standard on ~90% of targets — now runs operationally at NOAA (AIGFS, implemented December 17, 2025): 16-day forecasts, four runs a day, free on AWS. You get frontier AI weather without training anything. [14][15]
Free GRIB2 on the AWS Open Data registry; HRRR (3 km, hourly) covers CONUS mesoscale.
AIWP reforecast archive
NOAA
Archived GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, and FourCastNet hindcasts, with availability windows chosen to avoid each model's training period — ready-made honest inputs for backtesting the loop. [16]
Free on AWS. Open-Meteo offers a free API over GFS/HRRR/GraphCast plus ERA5 history for cheap prototyping. [17]
Flood Hub & open hydrology
Google Research
Riverine forecasts to 7 days across 150+ countries, plus a newly open-sourced flash-flood dataset and hydrology framework — the flood signal for the global phase. [1]
Public site + API access for partners; open dataset on Zenodo.

E · The risk engine

CLIMADA
ETH Zurich
Open-source (GPL, Python) probabilistic platform computing risk as hazard × exposure × vulnerability, event-based, globally consistent from 10 km down to ~100 m — and efficient enough for a laptop. Includes tropical-cyclone wind fields along synthetic tracks and an adaptation cost-benefit module; EIOPA builds EU insurance stress tools on it. This is the engine the agent should drive, extend, and calibrate rather than reinvent. [7]
github.com/CLIMADA-project — active scientific community; your county-probability layer and agent harness would be a novel contribution on top.

1.8 billion buildings — and none of them in the United States.

Open Buildings' coverage map is the single most important dataset fact for sequencing this roadmap. [3]

04 · The agentic experimental loop

The Claude Agent SDK was built for exactly this cycle

Anthropic's design principle for the SDK is to give the agent a computer — a filesystem, a terminal, and tools — because that is what lets it work the way a scientist does: find data, write and run code, inspect the result, and try again. The SDK (Python and TypeScript) ships the loop that powers Claude Code: gather context → take action → verify work → repeat, plus the machinery around it — subagents with isolated context windows for parallel work, automatic compaction for long runs, custom tools, hooks and permissions, and MCP for standardized connections to external data. Anthropic explicitly lists deep-research agents among its core use cases. [4][5]

The SDK loop, mapped onto disaster-risk forecasting

Gather context

MCP connectors and tools pull Storm Events, NRI, footprints, and forecast grids into a versioned workspace; the agent greps and samples files instead of loading everything into context.

→

Take action

The agent writes and runs code: fits hazard-frequency models, drives CLIMADA, produces a probability surface — e.g. P(damaging flood event, county × next 90 days) — as a versioned artifact.

→

Verify work

A deterministic harness — not the LLM — scores the surface against held-out history: Brier, skill vs. climatology, calibration by bin. Rules-based feedback is the strongest kind, per Anthropic's own guidance.

→

Repeat

The agent reads the score report, writes an experiment card (what changed, why, result), adjusts features or calibration, and reruns — stopping when the pre-registered threshold is met.

Subagents are the scaling mechanism. Risk work is embarrassingly parallel: one hazard-analyst subagent per peril (flood, hurricane wind, wildfire, heat) and per region, each with its own context window, returning only fitted parameters and scores to the orchestrator. A separate calibration-critic subagent reviews experiment cards and proposes the next iteration; a data-steward subagent watches for schema drift and stale sources. This is the SDK's stated purpose for subagents: parallelism plus context isolation. [4]

The precedent that agentic science loops work is real — and recent. Google's Co-Scientist, a multi-agent system that generates, critiques, tournament-ranks, and evolves hypotheses, was published in Nature in May 2026 with wet-lab-validated results in drug repurposing and liver fibrosis. Two design choices from that work transfer directly: every generated claim carries traceable citations, and the system is explicitly scientist-in-the-loop — outputs are starting points, not conclusions. Your loop is narrower and more checkable than open-ended hypothesis generation, because the verifier is a fixed statistical harness rather than expert judgment. [21]

The discipline that keeps the loop honest is separation of powers: the agent may propose any model it likes, but it cannot touch the harness, the holdout years, or the threshold. Lock test years (say, train ≤2015, validate 2016–2020, final test 2021–2025, touched once); pre-register the threshold in the repo before iterating; and when using AI-weather hindcasts, respect training-overlap windows — the NOAA archive publishes availability periods specifically chosen to avoid each model's training data. [16] "Iterate until accuracy is acceptable" is only science if the thing measuring accuracy never moves.

"Gather context → take action → verify work → repeat."

Anthropic's description of the agent feedback loop the SDK ships. [4]

Agent SDK vs. plain API

The client SDK gives you a model call; you own the loop. The Agent SDK gives you the loop — tool execution, context management, subagents, compaction — as a library. For a system meant to run unattended experiment cycles, start with the Agent SDK. [5]
05 · Architecture & the meaning of "accurate"

Four planes, one contract: the agent proposes, the harness disposes

Data plane

MCP servers / tools per source: storm-events, openfema-nri, footprints, forecast-grids. Each snapshots raw pulls with checksums, so every experiment is reproducible against a pinned data version.

Agent plane

Claude Agent SDK orchestrator + subagents (hazard analysts, calibration critic, data steward). Skills are markdown runbooks the agent reads: the CLIMADA recipe, the verification protocol, the experiment-card format.

Eval plane

Deterministic scoring harness with zero LLM involvement: locked holdouts, Brier / BSS / reliability / AUC, an append-only experiment ledger. The agent has read access and no write access.

Product plane

County risk briefs with uncertainty shown; later, the planning thought-partner that turns validated probabilities plus facility data into scenario stress-tests and draft plans, every line cited.

Define the forecast unit first. The tractable MVP unit is binary and auditable: "at least one damaging event of hazard H in county C within the next quarter," with damage defined by a fixed Storm Events threshold. County-quarter units give you ~3,100 counties × 4 quarters × 30 years of labeled history per hazard — enough to fit and to validate honestly. Finer resolution (tract, building, week) comes after the coarse unit is calibrated.

Score it the way meteorology does. The Brier score — mean squared error between forecast probability and the 0/1 outcome — has been the standard since 1950 and is strictly proper: the agent cannot game it by hedging. It decomposes (Murphy, 1973) into reliability (do predicted 20%s happen 20% of the time?), resolution (does the forecast distinguish situations?), and uncertainty (the base rate, outside anyone's control). The reliability diagram plots forecast probability against observed frequency; the diagonal is perfect calibration. [19]

"Within an acceptable threshold" then becomes a contract, not a feeling — versioned in the repo before iteration begins. A defensible starting contract: Brier Skill Score > 0 against a 20-year climatological baseline for every hazard (raw Brier scores are not comparable across hazards with different base rates); reliability within ±5 percentage points in every populated bin; AUC ≥ 0.7. Beating climatology sounds modest; most systems that skip this discipline quietly fail it. [19]

06 · Roadmap

Five phases, each with a falsifiable exit

Sequenced so every phase ships something usable and every claim of progress is a number on the locked harness. Phases 0–2 build the forecaster; 3 builds the thought-partner; 4 goes global.

0

Harness before model

Build the eval plane first, with no forecasting at all: Storm Events ingestion, county-quarter event labels for one hazard (inland flood) in one state, locked train/validate/test splits, Brier/BSS/reliability implementations, the experiment-card format, and the climatology baseline the agent must beat. Wire the Agent SDK skeleton (orchestrator + one subagent + MCP connector) and have it run the baseline end-to-end unattended.

Data: NOAA Storm Events; FEMA NRI (prior). Effort signal: weeks, not months — it's plumbing.

Exit when

The agent reproduces the climatology baseline's scores bit-for-bit from a clean clone, and the harness rejects a deliberately leaked model (a canary test).
1

The loop, on one hazard

Turn the agent loose on the experimental loop for flood risk statewide: feature construction from NRI, historical frequencies, terrain and precipitation reanalysis (ERA5 via Open-Meteo); candidate models from logistic regression up to gradient boosting; CLIMADA integration for event-based structure. The agent iterates — gather, act, verify, repeat — logging every experiment card, until the pre-registered contract is met on validation years. Then, once, the test years.

Data: + ERA5/Open-Meteo history, AIWP reforecasts, CLIMADA. Output: public backtest report — the project's credibility artifact.

Exit when

BSS > 0 vs. climatology on untouched test years (2021–2025) with reliability within ±5 pts per bin — published, with the ledger, in the open repo.
2

Multi-hazard, national, with exposure

Parallelize with subagents: hurricane wind, wildfire, tornado, heat — each hazard a subagent running the same loop against the same harness, nationwide. Join validated county probabilities to the exposure layer (USA Structures + Microsoft footprints) so outputs become human: "a 12% chance this quarter of an event that would touch ~3,400 structures, including 2 schools and a hospital." Publish as a free county risk brief.

Data: + USA Structures, Microsoft footprints, HURDAT2 tracks, live AIGFS feeds for nowcast-adjusted quarters.

Exit when

≥4 hazards pass the contract nationally; exposure joins are spot-validated against county assessor counts in 10 sampled counties.
3

The planning thought-partner

The evolution you described: an agent that takes a facility or jurisdiction (address, occupancy, dependencies) plus the validated risk layer, and interrogates its emergency plan the way §2's checklist interrogates Memorial's — scenario stress-tests (96-hour isolation, generator failure at surge, simultaneous hazards), gap reports, and draft plan sections grounded in the historical case library (Memorial and successors). Human emergency managers review everything; the agent's job is to make the analysis cheap, not to be in charge. Case studies become regression tests: any plan the system blesses must survive the scenario that killed people at Memorial.

Data: + case-study corpus (after-action reports, Fink's reporting), facility records supplied by the user; FEMA planning guidance as the template baseline.

Exit when

Blinded review: practicing emergency managers rate agent gap-reports for ≥3 real facilities as useful-or-better, and every recommendation traces to a source or a computed risk number.
4

Global scale-out

Swap the US-specific layers for their global counterparts — Google Open Buildings (+ 2.5D Temporal) for exposure, Flood Hub and the open hydrology framework for flood signal, EM-DAT and national records for ground truth — and rerun the same loop in two pilot countries chosen with a partner NGO (the anticipatory-action orgs already operating there are natural allies). The architecture doesn't change; the connectors do. This is where the mission lands: regions with the least analyst capacity are exactly where Open Buildings has coverage and local risk consultancy doesn't exist.

Data: Open Buildings, Flood Hub, EM-DAT, ERA5. Partner motion: GiveDirectly/IRC-style anticipatory programs as first users.

Exit when

The Phase 1 contract passes in two non-US pilots using only globally available data, and one partner organization uses the output operationally.
07 · Risks & open questions

Where this can go wrong

This is decision support, never a warning channel

Official alerts come from NWS and IPAWS. The product must say so on every surface, and quarterly probabilities must never be phrased as predictions of specific events. Getting this boundary wrong is the fastest way to cause harm and lose trust.

Overfitting the loop to history

"Iterate until the score clears" is a recipe for memorizing the past unless holdouts are locked, thresholds pre-registered, and the test set touched once. The harness's independence from the agent is the whole safety argument — treat any agent write-access to it as a security bug.

Ground truth is biased and drifting

Storm Events reflects reporting practice as much as weather: coverage and procedures changed over decades, and damage figures are estimates. [10] Fit on stable windows, model reporting effort where possible, and treat pre-1996 records (when many event types standardized) with suspicion. A changing climate also means history under-states tail risk — say so in outputs.

LLM text in life-safety documents

Numbers come from the harness; prose comes from the model — and prose can hallucinate. Require every plan sentence to cite either a computed number, a dataset row, or a named guidance document, validated by rules before display; route drafts through human sign-off. Co-Scientist's "traceable citations + expert-in-the-loop" pattern is the right template. [21]

Licensing hygiene

Microsoft footprints and parts of Open Buildings are ODbL (share-alike); other layers are CC BY-4.0 or public domain. Keep derived databases' licenses compatible from day one — retrofitting attribution stacks after mixing is painful. Publish a per-layer license manifest in the repo.

Publishing risk can harm the places you're helping

Address-level risk scores affect property values and insurance in ways that can hit vulnerable owners hardest. Default to county/tract aggregation publicly; reserve finer resolution for the facility's own planning sessions. Decide this policy before Phase 2, not after.

Federal data continuity

The Billion-Dollar Disasters retirement (May 2025, "evolving priorities, statutory mandates, and staffing changes") shows core series can stop. [20] Snapshot every dependency with checksums, mirror what licenses allow, and design connectors so a community-maintained substitute can slot in.
